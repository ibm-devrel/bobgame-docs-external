Agents and Non-determinism

Agent cognition vs. human cognition: giving the agent the map it can't see, and helping it revise its beliefs.

Present full screen ↗Click the slides (or ← →) to advance · S speaker notes · F fullscreen
Learning Objectives

By the end of this module you will:

  • Understand why games (and hidden-information problems in general) are hard for agents
  • Learn about six seeming LLM invariants, and how each one shows up in Deep Blue Drive Heist
  • Know the difference between deterministic automation and fully semantic (LLM-based) automation
  • Be able to "provide the map": inject the context an agent needs to make good decisions
  • Be able to apply belief revision, so the agent reconciles contradictions in its notebook

Goal: a better understanding of how to apply agentic techniques to more complicated, ambiguous, real-world problems.

Introduction

Games: As humans, we use them as a form of fun, but also as a way to play with intelligence. When humans play games, we do a lot of things:

  • Quickly internalize the rules of a new world
  • Set our entire "belief systems" around a newly-defined goal for the duration of the game
  • Design strategies to solve them effectively
  • Adjust those strategies as we find out more implicit rules of the world that aren't explicitly shared at the beginning

For the duration of the game, we're able to set aside everything we know, and simply believe in the game. When things shift, we adjust: entirely and fully. We can put down the old and move on with the new.

In this module, we'll explore why many types of games are so hard for agents to solve, and start building the intuitions for what agents are doing that is different from what we do.

The goal of this section is that you walk away with clear intuitions about the shape of problems that it's reasonable to apply agents to.

The ARC-AGI-3 Prize

Consider the ARC-AGI-3 benchmark. This competition is a series of puzzles created by game designers. (They're quite fun: Try one out for yourself! There's a "human" mode.)

The measurement isn't just "can you solve it". LLMs can solve the puzzle. The measurement, instead, is: given no information about the rules, can you internalize the rules and apply them?

Humans figure this out in a few moves, but agents do much worse - even relatively advanced ones. Humans regularly beat LLMs at this task.

The reason for this has to do with the way LLMs "think". Even though the output looks like what humans create, the LLM arrives at its conclusions very differently.

Note

This particular task isn't looked highly on in the research community. It's not necessarily the best benchmark for LLMs. Instead, it illustrates an important point that we can use in our AI agent development.

Agents Playing Minecraft

In October 2026, a team from Google published a paper in which they set a team of Gemini 4 Argon agents to beat the main "storyline" in Minecraft: defeating the Ender Dragon. Their agents succeeded after 27 hours.

There are several things to note about their approach:

  • Each step in the storyline was verifiable. The agents were able to tell whether or not they were making progress.
  • It took 4 agents to make it through the full story.
  • The agents had full access to a coding workspace, where they would write scripts to support their progress and strategize.
  • The agents coordinated using in-game chat.
  • While the agents weren't given access to strategy documents, the LLMs underlying the agents saw many papers on Minecraft strategy during pretraining.
  • The agents died frequently - having access only to a Python SDK to control their characters, they didn't seem to "learn" much over the course of the game about how not to make bad decisions.
  • Agents spent most of their coordination time mapping the world: writing down coordinates of themselves, important items they discovered, and where there were traps.
  • Agents spent very little of their overall communication time developing a better strategy (11/229 messages).
  • The win required 1.35 billion input tokens, and 16 million output tokens: around $520 worth of compute.

We can learn a lot from this example, which represents a state-of-the-art approach to agent development.

Deep Blue Drive Heist is a much smaller game than Minecraft, and it's still hard: about 1 in 10 players have built an agent that solves the full 26-city game on its own. In this module, we'll look at why, and what you can do about it.

The Concept: The LLM Invariants

LLMs keep getting bigger and more capable. But as long as they stay roughly in the space of the transformer architecture, some of their limitations don't go away. We will call these LLM invariants. This isn't a formal term: It's intended to be a teaching concept. Any one of them might be "broken" in further testing and benchmarks, if any one of them were taken as a goal of LLM training instead of a side effect. However, under current paradigms, these aren't optimized for directly - and so they serve as seams between LLMs and humans.

There are six of them:

  1. LLMs can't map the world
  2. LLMs must operate on verification loops
  3. LLMs exhibit stochastic (random) behavior
  4. LLMs can't revise beliefs in one session
  5. LLMs won't shift objectives in one session
  6. Attention follows frequency

Underneath all six: an LLM will focus on responding to the most recent piece of information, rather than taking a step back to see the forest for the trees.

The ability to notice when it is time to take a step back and regroup appears to be fairly uniquely human.

You can work around every one of these. But you have to know they're there first. When one agent works with a human watching over it, the human quietly papers over these problems, and they're easy to miss. When an agent works on its own (like the player agent) or many agents work together, they get in the way of the work.

1. Can't map the world

At least for now, LLMs don't appear to build "models" of the world the way we do. This is easiest to see in how they fail to reason about the physical world. Colin Fraser asked an early video model for "a dog jumps through a hoop":

We can see in this video that the LLM doesn't have an understanding of the physics that it's demonstrating: It reads "through" and allows the dog to simply phase through the hoop.

Models have advanced since then, but elements of this behavior still exist in the most advanced models.

There is a variant of this in software architecture and math: LLMs don't understand conceptual architectures as "constraints" the way a human might.

A human who designs a "model-view-controller" pattern, for example, and knows how to use it, might notice every time they want to add something that breaks the pattern. (Inexperienced people won't, but you'd expect an experienced person to do this.)

An LLM will simply echo what appears to be the pattern, if it gets it closer to its goals.

Each piece of what the model produces can look right, while the whole doesn't hang together.

Building Deep Blue Drive Heist involved a lot of graph generation algorithms. Despite describing the algorithms several different ways, the LLM coding agent was unable to translate them without seeing a direct pseudocode implementation.

This is because even when we discussed it in English, it did not have a very strong conceptual understanding of the task at hand. It only understood the words I used, and made rough associations.

Humans who actually understand an algorithm or formula on a deep level may make translation / encoding errors when writing code, but they're unlikely to make conceptual errors that demonstrate a lack of understanding of the concept.

Real-world agentic example

Consider the example of using a voice assistant backed by an LLM to help manage household tasks - something I did recently.

By default, the agent wanted to tell me to parallelize laundry: get it all done at once!

It did not understand that once the washing machine was in use, I couldn't put more into it until that load was done and time had passed.

To get any real help out of it, I had to describe the systems in my house: what state each one can be in, and what steps move it from one state to the next.

A Model's Context Window, In Context

Part of the problem is the size of the window the model has on the world. A 1-million-token context window holds about 4 MB of text, or roughly three floppy disks. Humanity created around 180 zettabytes of data in 2025: about 22 one-terabyte laptops' worth for every person on Earth, in a single year.

So the model has a tiny lens, and no map. Whatever it knows about the world it's working in, you have to put through that lens.

In the game

The player agent can't see the map on the dashboard. It doesn't know how many cities there are, where it is relative to the finale, or where it's been unless it wrote it down. All it gets is what the narrator says, what the NPC says, what its tools return, and its notebook. You can see the map. The agent can't.

In your codebase

A coding agent has the same problem with your repo and your org. Without a map of the code (tools like CodeGraph or tree-sitter) or of how your organization works (internal wikis, AGENTS.md), it can't make good decisions about where a change belongs.

2. Operate on verification loops

Because the model doesn't have a map, the way to keep it on track is to check its work. Agents can only make progress when they have a deterministic checking algorithm they can run, or something else tangible they can directly observe.

In September 2026, a swarm of about 10,000 agents produced a proof for (a forced, 3D version of) the Navier–Stokes problem, one of the $1 million Millennium Prize Problems. The proof was formalized in Lean, a language that a computer can check.

The Lean language allowed the agents to come up with a solution and make progress: checking every step.

Humans may use this sort of tool as well, but they will generally have a strong sense of whether they are right first.

The crucial bit of verification that must still be done by humans is to guarantee that the statement being shown to be true in Lean is logically equivalent to what mathematicians set out to prove.

— Quanta, "AI Has Solved One of Math's $1 Million Millennium Prize Problems," Sep 8, 2026

The Minecraft run worked the same way. Each of the thirteen steps from bare hands to the Ender Dragon was something the agents could check, and a fifth "Observer" agent checked their work.

In the game

The clearest verification loop is enter_evidence_words at the finale (see Module 1). The safe tells the agent how many of its words were right.

3. Stochastic behavior

LLMs are probabilistic machines, by definition. Even if you tune the weights very strongly, 1 time in 100, 2 + 2 might come out as 5.

Keep in mind

This is an intentional simplification. Real networks are more interpretable than "a bag of random weights": see Neural Networks Are Already Clean.

The same agent, with the same prompt, will make different decisions on different runs. "Intelligence" alone will not change this. This is fundamental to understanding what agents will do.

Sometimes the difference is small. Sometimes one run goes straight to the drive and the next one wanders between two cities until it runs out of time.

In the game

Never judge a change to your agent by one run. A good run might be luck, and a bad one might be too.

4. Can't revise beliefs

Given two conflicting pieces of information, an LLM usually won't stop to work out which one is true. Instead, it will flip back and forth between acting as if one is true and acting as if the other is. Or it will say "you said A and B, so therefore…" and invent a new state of reality where both can be true at once.

Their "cognition" is additive. Every new message adds to what the model is working with, and it can't easily go back to an earlier state. Crossing something out is not a thing it does well.

This is the same thing ARC-AGI-3 tests. To learn a game's rules as you play, you have to notice that your guess about how the game works was wrong, and replace it. Humans do this constantly without thinking about it. LLMs struggle with it.

In the game

The agent treats its notebook as fact. But the notebook was written by an LLM, and it has probably hallucinated somewhere along the way: a lead recorded with the wrong destination, a note about a city it never visited. Once that's in the notebook, the agent will keep building on it.

5. Can't shift objectives

Once an LLM starts down a path, it's hard to redirect. If you start a refactor shaped one way, then change your mind halfway through the conversation, you are better off starting a new session than trying to keep steering the old one to the new plan.

An LLM will not reliably recognize that what you asked for under the first plan should be thrown out under the second.

Stochastic behavior makes this worse when more than one agent is involved. Agents make different decisions every time they start, so four people giving agents roughly the same prompt (worded slightly differently) will end up with systems built in very different ways. Drift like that inside a single system can be catastrophic. A simple example: agent 1 writes a config reader, agent 2 writes a config reader. Which one wins?

Agents tend to produce solutions by committee. One builds the entrance to the bridge, one builds the supports, one lays the asphalt. They try to make the seams line up, run a load test, and declare success.

(Humans exhibit this too, when coordinating in groups. But intelligent experts will usually notice when one piece is out of place.)

Agents can simulate what a strong and coordinated team of humans do if you help guide them towards a shared plan and strategy.

In the game

The engine resets the player agent's conversation at every city. That's a feature: the agent gets a fresh start, and can't double down on whatever it was fixated on in the last city.

But anything wrong in its notebook will persist, and in the new city, the agent may treat it like truth.

6. Attention follows frequency

Agents weigh how true something is by how often they see it. The more times an agent session hears something, the more likely it is to act as though it's true.

Frequency isn't the only explanation for how LLMs behave. But it does explain why, put in the right probability space, an LLM will passionately defend a flat earth.

In the game

If the agent writes the same wrong lead into its notes three times, the wrong lead wins. It doesn't matter that one correct lead pointed somewhere else.

How might you correct this issue?

Graph-Based vs. Semantic Agents

Knowing the invariants, how do you build an agent that works anyway? One of the biggest choices is how much of the agent's behavior you leave up to the LLM.

A fully semantic agent is the general-purpose ReAct loop from Module 1. The LLM decides everything: which tool to call next, when to stop, when to answer. Most coding agents work this way. This flexibility comes at a cost when approaching problems where agents struggle.

Using graphs to guide an LLM's behavior allows you to change the deterministic structure. In LangGraph, you build the agent as a graph of nodes and edges. The code decides who runs when, what each node can see, and when the state resets. The semantic decision-making is limited to when it is needed.

Deep Blue Drive Heist uses a graph to coordinate the agents. The engine decides when the narrator speaks, when the NPC replies, and when the conversation resets. The player agent only decides what to do on its own turn, from the tools it has. That structure takes care of some of the invariants for you:

Invariant What the game already does What's left to you
Can't map the world Gives the agent its connections, visited_cities, and known_routes at each city Building a bigger picture of the map than the current city
Operate on verification loops enter_evidence_words at the finale Designing a strategy which uses enter_evidence_words for verification loops
Stochastic behavior Means LLMs will produce different results on each city and run Using hooks, prompts and the notebook to enforce guardrails around an algorithm
Can't revise beliefs Provides a notebook Create a strategy for the agent to ensure it doesn't have conflicts and bad information in the notebook
Can't shift objectives Resets the conversation at every city Use the prompt, hooks and notebook to force the agent to reconsider when stuck (and to notice when stuck)
Attention follows frequency Provides structures to store information Prevent wrong information from being repeated several times

How might we adjust our agent to better handle the invariants?

Step 1: Build the map

Start a game and look at the dashboard. Make a list of everything the dashboard tells you that the agent can't see. Your list might include:

  • The map. The agent doesn't know how many cities there are or where it is. It can only "see" what the narrator and its notebook tell it.
  • How long it's been. It can't tell whether it should be rushing, and it can't see the path it's taken unless it wrote it down.
  • Its own loops. It can't see that it's been going back and forth between the same two cities.

This information is all right there for the agent. A human would notice the rules, write down useful information in their notes, and offer strategy for the next round.

But remember our Minecraft agents? Who only communicated new strategy 11 out of 229 times?

Your agent is the same. If you want it to create a plan to cross the map, you need to tell it how.

Exercise · Teach Bob what the agent can't see

First, tell Bob. Bob doesn't know what the dashboard shows you, and it doesn't know what the player agent can't see. Ask it to add a section to AGENTS.md, so it keeps this in mind for the rest of your session (and in future sessions).

Bob · Agent modeAdd a section to AGENTS.md called "Creating the map".

"Human contestants can see the full map of cities, how long it's been, and when the agent is cycling. That information isn't clearly revealed to the agent. Suggest strategies that might surface that information more clearly to an agent."

Next, brainstorm with Bob. Bob is an LLM too, so it can use itself as a test bed: give it only what the player agent would get, and ask what it would need.

Bob · Plan modeWhat can we do to expose the map better to the player agent? Pretend you're the player agent arriving in a new city, using only what it saw in a recent log in logs/. Then suggest how build_system_prompt or the briefing could use the orientation (connections, visited_cities, known_routes) to show it the map.
One way to draw the map

A short, structured block at the top of the system prompt goes a long way. For example:

cities:
  armonk:    toronto, dublin
  toronto:   armonk, dublin, amsterdam
  dublin:    armonk, toronto, amsterdam, stockholm

visited:  armonk -> toronto
location: toronto

The agent only learns a city's connections by visiting it, so the map has to be built up as it goes. The orientation's known_routes already holds every route it has seen so far: the default prompt just never shows it to the agent.

Once you're happy with the plan, switch to Agent mode and have Bob implement it.

Step 2: Belief revision

The agent treats its notebook as factual information about the world. But it's an LLM, and it has probably hallucinated somewhere along the way. How can we get it to change its beliefs, and reconcile the information so it has a clearer view of the world at each city?

Take a look at the notebook tools the agent has: record_lead, record_evidence, take_note, check_notebook, revise, and cross_out. The agent can add to its notebook, read it back, correct an entry or mark it confirmed (revise), and strike out an entry it no longer trusts (cross_out). But having the tools isn't the same as using them: left alone, the agent rarely goes back to fix what it wrote.

  • In the system prompt, add a step at every city: read the notebook, look for contradictions, and decide what's true before doing anything else.
  • Instruct the agent to try to compute which direction several leads are pointing, select a hypothesis, and cross out entries pointing in the wrong direction, or that might be leading to cycle behavior.
  • Your code can read the notebook (self.notebook), but it can't change it. Use the briefing or a highlight hook to point out problems for the agent to fix: leads grouped by destination, a chess piece that points at two different cities, or a lead nothing has confirmed yet.
Exercise · Reconcile the notebook

Explain belief revision to Bob, and plan the change with it.

Bob · Plan modeThe player agent treats its notebook as fact, but the notebook was written by an LLM and probably contains mistakes: leads with the wrong destination, notes about things that didn't happen, the same lead written down several times.

LLMs are bad at belief revision: when two pieces of information conflict, they flip between them instead of deciding which one is true.

Plan how the player agent can revise its beliefs at every city: how the system prompt should tell it to find contradictions and decide what's true, when it should use revise and cross_out, and how the briefing or a highlight hook could read self.notebook and point out contradictions and duplicates for it to fix. Check a recent log in logs/ for examples of mistakes in the notebook.

Then switch to Agent mode and have Bob implement the plan.

What's Working

Your agent now arrives at each city with more than its notebook. It has a map of what it's seen so far, a sense of how long it's been searching, and a warning when it's going in circles. And it no longer takes its notebook at face value: at every city, it checks what it wrote down before it acts on it.

What's Still Hard

The agent is still stochastic. Two runs of the same agent can look very different, so it's hard to tell whether a change made things better or you just got lucky. And every change you make now touches the system prompt, the hooks, and the notebook all at once. When the drive-recovery time goes down, which change did it?
